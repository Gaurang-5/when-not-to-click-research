When Not to Click: Auditing Native Visual Confidence under GUI Target Ambiguity and Distribution Shift
Dr. Sowmya K S, Garvit Mundra, Gaurang Bhatia
B.M.S. College Of Engineering
ABSTRACT
A graphical user interface (GUI) agent needs to establish that a requested control is present and uniquely identified before deciding where to click. We examine the native scores returned by a pinned visual interface, Jev 1.14: a visible-presence probability, a match-count distribution and one bounding box per request. These scores form the basis of frozen selective-execution policies. Presence and uniqueness gates made no incorrect accepted clicks on held-out synthetic layouts. The result did not carry over to real interfaces. A source audit found that 200 of the 951 human-labelled cases reused pilot screenshots. Among the 751 independent real-interface cases, the three-signal policy accepted 26 with a conditional risk of 61.5%; the frozen logistic gate accepted one. Strict localization succeeded on 41 of 131 unique targets (31.3%), limiting any gate to about 6.1% coverage at 10% accepted-error risk. Recalibration on real data improved Brier loss, but no threshold met the 10% risk target. A quantized Qwen2.5-VL-7B baseline tested on matched inputs achieved 16.8% strict accuracy. We contribute a reproducible audit of transfer failure, the dependence of safety metrics on their denominators, and cohort provenance. We do not introduce a new uncertainty-control algorithm.
Keywords: GUI grounding; selective prediction; abstention; calibration; distribution shift; vision–language models
Introduction
Knowing where a model would click does not tell us whether the requested control exists or whether it has been identified uniquely. An absent target calls for a different decision from a duplicated, occluded or offscreen target. The pinned native visual interface studied here returns a presence score, a caller-defined match-count distribution and one bounding box in the same request. We examine these outputs through four research questions:
RQ1. Do native presence and uniqueness scores reduce incorrect accepted clicks relative to box confidence on held-out synthetic layouts?
RQ2. How well do the frozen policies transfer to human-labelled real interfaces?
RQ3. How do coverage, localization, calibration, latency and cost change across sources and target states?
RQ4. How does a frozen local vision–language model (VLM) baseline perform on the same images and human references?
Our contribution is an audit that can be reproduced: it examines a native model interface, documents the limits of transfer and includes a baseline evaluated on matched inputs. It does not propose a new uncertainty-control algorithm.
The related-work section places the study in its research context. Materials and methods describes the policies, data, annotation and evaluation protocol. Results reports the transfer, calibration and model comparisons; Discussion interprets them. Conclusion sets out the claims supported by the evidence.
Related work
OS-ATLAS develops cross-platform GUI grounding and action modelling (Wu et al. 2024). SafeGround uses stochastic spatial dispersion and calibration to control false discoveries (Wang, Fan, and Wang 2026). Argus compares uncertainty methods across models and interfaces, and finds that transfer depends on the observable regime (Kumar et al. 2026). CORA learns an action-risk guardian and calibrates execute/abstain behaviour (Feng et al. 2026).
Selective classification (Geifman and El-Yaniv 2017), Learn then Test (Angelopoulos et al. 2021) and Conformal Risk Control (Angelopoulos et al. 2024) supply the foundational rejection and risk-control framework. Qwen2.5-VL (Qwen Team 2025), ScreenSpot-Pro (Li et al. 2025) and UI-TARS (Qin et al. 2025) provide additional GUI-model and benchmark context. Together these establish prior art for abstention, calibration and GUI safety.
We focus on the three native scores exposed by Jev 1.14 and on what a synthetic-to-real evaluation can establish. Appendix D includes published VLM results for context. Those results were not obtained using our labels.
Materials and methods
Native confidence and selective execution
All primary-study requests use the pinned model version jev-1.14.0 and prompt presence-count-box-v1. The separate protocol for repeated requests and prompt variants is specified in Appendix C.4.
For case i, the interface returns three scores:
pᵢ ∈ [0, 1], the visible-presence confidence;
qᵢ = (qᵢ,₀, qᵢ,₁, qᵢ,>₁), a probability distribution over match counts (“Choice”), with uᵢ = qᵢ,₁ the probability of exactly one match;
bᵢ ∈ [0, 1], the box confidence attached to the predicted box B̂ᵢ.
The parser rejects malformed probabilities, unexpected model versions, non-finite coordinates and degenerate boxes. Let vᵢ = 1 if the output parses validly and vᵢ = 0 otherwise. A failed output counts as an abstention for every policy and stays in all evaluation denominators. The selected Choice label, argmaxₖ qᵢ,ₖ, is used only to route rejected cases to clarification or abstention. No interactive clarification was executed.
Each policy maps the native scores to an acceptance score g and compares it with a threshold τ (Table 1). Policy E is an L2-regularised logistic regression on the three native scores, with no interaction terms (Equation 1). Its coefficients β₀ = −4.496, βₚ = 2.509, βᵤ = 2.696 and β_b = 0.600 were fitted to the 79 valid development responses.

Data, annotation and provenance
Synthetic pilot. We generated procedural geometry labels for 25 templates spanning five layout families, with eight state variants for each template. Development uses 80 cases from sidebar and kanban layouts; calibration uses 40 table cases; the held-out test uses 80 dialog and toolbar cases. Each template supplies three unique cases and one case in each of the absent, multiple, occluded, offscreen and unclear states. We made 200 calls without retries. Of these, 199 parsed successfully; a zero-width box in the development split failed closed.
Annotation. Two annotators labelled the same 951 cases independently, without access to model predictions. They assigned one of six target states to each case, drew boxes around every valid visible target in unique and multiple cases, and recorded their confidence. Structural validation passed for both corrected exports.
Status agreement is 759/951 (79.8%), with Cohen’s κ = 0.613.
Mean matched box IoU is 0.619 across 205 comparable cases.
We flag cases deterministically for status disagreement, disagreement about the number of boxes, matched IoU below 0.5, or low confidence. This identifies 259 cases; a case may have more than one reason for being flagged. For these cases, the authors directed that the reviewer-approved Annotator B decision be used as final. The remaining 692 cases use the consensus status and, when localization is possible, the mean of matched box coordinates.
Source audit. We checked source provenance offline and found that 200 of the 951 human-labelled cases repeated both the screenshot bytes and the instructions from the pilot. They comprise 80 development, 40 calibration and 80 held-out cases. Since these repeats cannot provide independent external evidence, we treat the full 951-case collection as descriptive and report it as a mixed cohort. We analyse the 751 ScreenSpot-v2-variant real-interface cases separately (ScreenSpot-v2-variants n.d.; Figure 1).
We added this subset analysis after examining the mixed results. It is therefore a post-hoc correction of provenance, not a new preregistered experiment. Humans rechecked the negative instructions proposed by the source; 131 received a final label of unique. This finding explains why the automatic negative labels could not be used on their own. The subset should not be confused with the standard ScreenSpot-v2 test set of positive targets.

Evaluation and uncertainty protocol
We retain the original experimental policy definitions. Evaluation covers strict localization (Equation 4), secondary centre-only accuracy, accepted-error risk and marginal error (Equation 6), acceptance within each target state, and useful automation among unique cases.
We form confidence intervals from 2,000 nonparametric bootstrap resamples of source-image clusters, using seed 1729. Instructions referring to the same screenshot stay together during resampling. We recompute each ratio in every resample, omit undefined replicates and retain the number omitted. The primary paired comparisons use 10,000 randomizations that flip source-cluster effects. Within each cohort, Holm adjustment covers the absent and multiple endpoints.
The real-only comparisons are exploratory, as are the new subgroup, calibration, entropy and curve analyses. The adjusted absent-target p = .0476 is borderline within this exploratory family and does not support a central claim. Holm adjustment for two endpoints does not account for the full set of diagnostic analyses. We use percentile bootstrap intervals descriptively; with few accepted cases, they may be unreliable. Resampling an error-free set cannot introduce an unseen error, so a degenerate zero-error interval does not demonstrate zero true risk.

Calibration and decision analysis
Rejection quality must be considered alongside base localization. Strict accuracy on unique real targets is 31.3%; a centre-only rule gives 58.8%, and IoU ≥ 0.3 alone gives 48.9%. Even where both annotators label a real case unique, only 61.2% of their boxes agree at IoU ≥ 0.5. This human agreement is a conditional reference, not an absolute ceiling on performance. Appendix C.1 gives the full sensitivity analysis for labels and scoring criteria.
Oracle coverage bound. For fixed recorded boxes and labels, let π̂_corr = (1/n) Σᵢ vᵢ yᵢ be the fraction of all cases with a strictly correct localization. Any gate accepts correct cases at a rate of at most π̂_corr (Equation 7a). If the gate’s observed conditional risk is at most a tolerance α, its total coverage is bounded by Equation (7b).

Here π̂_corr = 41/751 = 0.0546, so even an oracle gate is bounded by 0.0546/0.90 ≈ 6.1% coverage at α = 0.10 (Figure 3). This is a deterministic finite-cohort bound, not a population guarantee or a human ceiling; a different grounder or scoring rule changes it.

Figure 3. Frozen real-cohort operating points (coverage, conditional risk; Table 3) against the oracle frontier of Equation (7b). For a fixed set of recorded boxes, no gate can reach the shaded region, because only 41 of 751 cases are strictly correct. At α = 0.10, coverage is bounded by 6.1%. The observed policies sit far above the frontier, so better gating alone cannot recover useful low-risk automation.
Probability quality. Original E has a Brier loss of 0.06125. The retrospective constant-prevalence predictor does better at 0.05234, leaving E with Brier skill −0.170 (Equation C1). Its low expected calibration error (ECE) is therefore insufficient evidence of useful probabilities. Nor do the extra native signals show strong additional discrimination: B and E have nearly the same AUROC, with overlapping source-cluster intervals on the real cohort. Appendix C.2 reports the ranking and Brier results in full.
Real-data recalibration. In a diagnostic with separate source images for fitting, calibration and testing, we train a new logistic score using only the real fitting data. Test Brier loss falls from 0.07345 to 0.05822. Yet neither the empirical calibration-risk rule nor the fixed Learn-then-Test procedure identifies a threshold that meets 10% accepted-error risk. Better probability estimates do not, in this experiment, yield useful acceptance at low risk. The retrospective design and assumptions about source distributions prevent a new confirmatory guarantee. Appendix C.3 describes the procedure.
Strata. The appendix breaks down results by platform (desktop, mobile, web), target status, target area, element type, instruction length, annotator confidence and label provenance. For unique cases only, target size is the reference-box area divided by screenshot area: small is below 0.1%, medium is 0.1–1%, and large is at least 1%. We take annotator confidence to be the lower confidence of the two annotators. Results for sparse strata are descriptive; they play no part in policy tuning.

As standard decision-theoretic framing rather than a methodological contribution, we define a transparent utility: a correct accepted click earns one unit, an incorrect accepted click loses λ > 0 units, and a rejection earns zero. This is a hypothetical sensitivity model, not measured harm; dollar API cost and wall-clock latency are reported separately. With TP = Σᵢ aᵢyᵢ and FP = Σᵢ aᵢ(1 − yᵢ), the utility is Equation (9).

On real interfaces, Û_B = (17 − 28λ)/751, Û_D = (10 − 16λ)/751 and Û_E = 1/751. The pairwise break-even points are:
D exceeds B only when λ > 7/12;

The ranking is therefore B > D > E for λ < 9/16, and E > D > B for λ > 7/12. A completely abstaining policy has zero utility. These break-even points expose the coverage–risk trade-off rather than labelling one policy universally best (Figure 6).

Model comparison, latency and cost
For the independent Qwen2.5-VL-7B baseline, we use the same 951 prepared images and human references. All input-image hashes agree, and the pinned model/protocol hashes remain consistent throughout collection. We calculate primary accuracy on the 751 real cases, which include 131 unique targets.
The deployments differ in their interfaces as well as their execution. Qwen runs through Ollama with Q4_K_M quantization, a frozen prompt that forces a box in JSON, and prompted confidence values. Jev exposes its native typed interface. We do not apply the logistic coefficients trained on Jev to Qwen. Our comparison is limited to these deployment and interface choices.

Jev achieves 31.3% strict accuracy, compared with 16.8% for Qwen. The paired strict difference, Qwen minus Jev, is −14.5 percentage points, with a source-cluster 95% interval of −22.2 to −7.3. Under the centre-only criterion the accuracies are closer, at 58.8% and 51.1%, and the interval for their difference includes zero. These results favour the tested Jev interface within this cohort; they do not establish that the model is generally superior (Figure 7).
We also apply a common semantic rule: accept a valid output only if its predicted match-count label is one. For Jev, this accepts 194/751 cases, including 163 incorrect accepts. For Qwen, it accepts 604/751, including 583 incorrect accepts. The rule is deliberately simple and is evaluated separately from the frozen B–E policies. Its results show that the semantic “one” label is insufficient for reliable acceptance. Qwen’s prompted scores should not be interpreted as native calibrated probabilities.
Failures. All 62 Qwen failures contain out-of-bounds or degenerate boxes and fail closed; 57 belong to the real subset. Jev produces 11 malformed outputs in the original matched collection of 951, all within the real subset. We neither repair outputs nor rerun selected cases. The experiment compares the specified interfaces that force a box. It does not separate the contributions of model weights, quantization, structured decoding and coordinate prompting. Results may change with different optimization for each model.
Latency. For each deployment, we measure elapsed wall-clock time from the request until the full response arrives, including time spent on malformed responses. Hosted Jev and serial local Qwen run on different hardware and software stacks. Their measured latencies therefore do not constitute a comparison at matched hardware. We have not priced the local Qwen hardware or electricity; no API charge does not mean no economic cost. Appendix D retains published model results and GPT-4o price scenarios as historical context.
Collection and cost. The external collection records 951 HTTP-200 responses, preceded by 71 credential rejections. Among the 951 responses, 940 parse successfully and 11 fail closed: six have invalid boxes and five have invalid probability sums. We extracted usage even from raw responses that failed parsing, for totals of 3,199,297 input and 73,284 output tokens. The recorded Jev input price is $0.042 per million tokens, with output tokens listed as free (TypeSafe AI 2026). On that basis, estimated cost is $0.134370, or about $0.0001413 per HTTP-200 response. This estimate uses the published rate; it is not a provider invoice. The synthetic study cost approximately $0.019235. Across valid and malformed HTTP-200 responses, mean latency is 459 ms and p95 is 628 ms. Including setup rejections gives a mean of 456 ms.
Discussion
The study exposes a gap between discrimination on authored templates and usefulness on real interfaces. Native semantic confidence may reject clear synthetic duplicates while transferring poorly to small controls and underspecified real instructions. Near-universal abstention can also make marginal error look low. The provenance audit shows why a strong-looking mixed result needs to be separated by source before it can support a generalization claim.
Annotation limitations. Agreement on target status is below 80%. For flagged cases, the final labels follow reviewer-approved Annotator B decisions rather than decisions from a separate third adjudicator. This provenance cannot by itself establish the reliability of independent review. We have prepared a blinded audit package of 100 cases, comprising 60 flagged and 40 consensus cases, for a human reviewer. We do not claim any completed third-review results. Later label changes must be released as a new dataset version and analysed as a clearly identified sensitivity cohort.
Cohort limitations.
Absent targets dominate the real cohort (583/751). The unique labels come from human rechecking of instructions that were proposed as negatives.
The cohort covers mobile, web and desktop interfaces. Offscreen and occluded cases are rare, leaving too few observations for stable conclusions about those strata.
We cannot rule out training contamination because Jev’s public training data are unknown.
Clustering by source image accounts for repeated template screenshots, although dependence within applications may persist.
The subgroup diagnostics were devised after the mixed outcomes had been seen. No global correction for multiple comparisons was applied.
Scope of the expansion. The reviewer-driven extension adds a five-pass Jev study with two prompt variants and a collection from a pinned local Qwen2.5-VL baseline. Both are complete and reported in Appendix C. One quantized baseline cannot establish robustness across model families or deployment configurations. We provide no formal control of risk under distribution shift. The recorded incorrect clicks are offline outcomes, not measurements of user harm; no real application action was executed. Further evidence will require independent adjudication, new balanced cases from real interfaces, and tests spanning more models and configurations.
Conclusion
On authored synthetic layouts, native presence and uniqueness signals substantially improve selective clicking. The real-interface results do not establish comparable usefulness. Once the 200 reused pilot cases are removed, the frozen logistic gate accepts only one of 751 real cases, while conditional risk for the three-signal policy is still 61.5%. What the study supports is a reproducible account of failed transfer, cohort provenance and safety metrics whose interpretation depends on the denominator. Under the tested interface, the quantized Qwen baseline has weaker strict localization, and neither model’s semantic one-label rule provides reliable acceptance. We have not established broader model superiority, formal safety or practical utility.

C.3 Real-data recalibration
Partition. We assign source images to fitting, calibration and test sets deterministically with a seeded hash. A screenshot can appear in only one partition. The fit partition contains 296 cases from 292 source clusters; calibration contains 226 from 218 clusters; test contains 229 from 220 clusters. E_real is a new L2 logistic diagnostic trained only on valid fitting outputs. The original E remains frozen and is not replaced.
Learn then Test. For Learn then Test (Angelopoulos et al. 2021), the threshold grid is fixed at Λ = {0, 0.05, …, 1}, i.e. 21 thresholds. For threshold λⱼ ∈ Λ, let Kⱼ be the number of accepted calibration cases and Eⱼ the number of incorrect ones. We test the null hypothesis that conditional accepted-error risk is at least α = 0.10 with the one-sided exact binomial lower-tail p-value in Equation (C2), where δ = 0.05 and M = 126 (21 thresholds × 6 scores) applies Bonferroni correction. To address within-screenshot dependence, one case is independently hash-selected from every source cluster, using no labels. Certified thresholds maximise calibration acceptance within the passing family; if none passes, the procedure abstains.


(C2)
A threshold with Eⱼ = 0 passes only if 0.9^Kⱼ ≤ 0.05/126, i.e. only with Kⱼ ≥ 75 error-free accepts.
The binomial guarantees concern the sampled-cluster distribution. They depend on independent, identically distributed source clusters and a fixed calibration protocol. We cannot establish those assumptions fully after inspecting the data. Certification in this analysis is consequently a property of the stated diagnostic procedure, rather than a guarantee for deployment.
Empirical comparator. The comparator selects the greatest calibration acceptance with observed risk no higher than 10%; it provides no statistical guarantee. Under the strict criterion, neither this comparator nor LTT finds a qualifying threshold for any of the six scores. The recovery experiment therefore fails to demonstrate useful automation at the specified 10% risk target. Testing a different risk budget or correctness criterion would require a separate, explicitly identified analysis, rather than a choice made from test outcomes.

C.4 Repeatability and model comparison
Protocol. For the repeat study, we add four passes with the original prompt over the 751 real cases, giving five passes when the existing collection is included. Two prewritten prompt variants bring the plan to 4,506 additional requests. Each new request has a separate repeat/prompt ID and an immutable protocol hash. Once an ID has a non-auth attempt, it is not silently resent, including after a timeout or malformed output. All 7/7 pass/variant cohorts are complete at manuscript generation; headline metrics exclude partial collections.
Stability. Correctness changes across the five original-prompt passes in 15/751 cases. The mean per-case fraction assigned the modal match-count label is 0.976. The frozen acceptance decision changes for B in 26/751 cases, for D in 17/751 and for E in 1/751. Mean pairwise box IoU is 0.711 over 7,315 valid within-case output pairs; pairs containing malformed outputs are excluded. We retain between-pass metrics separately from per-case consistency. Repeated observations are never pooled as independent screenshots.
Additional collection. The completed extension contains 4,506 attempts and 67 fail-closed outputs. Recorded usage totals 16,457,448 input tokens and 346,928 output tokens. Including usage recorded for malformed responses, the estimated additional cost is $0.691213 at the recorded rate, with output tokens free. We did not automatically repeat any failed attempt.
Pass results. B accepts 43–47 cases across the five original-prompt passes, while D accepts 21–26. Conditional risk for D ranges from 57.1% to 71.4%; E accepts 0–1 cases. With the literal prompt, D accepts 24 and makes 15 errors. With the conservative prompt, it accepts 32 and makes 23 errors. Changing the prompt does not restore useful coverage at low risk with the frozen thresholds. These repeats describe variation within cases and add no independent test screens.

Timing definitions. Published local measurements distinguish time to first token (TTFT) from time per output token (TPOT). Our measurements instead cover the full HTTP response. For L output tokens, approximate local generation time is TTFT + (L − 1) × TPOT. The selected study gives no comparable timing for GPT-4o, so it cannot establish a Jev speedup.
GPT-4o price scenario. The official input/output prices are $2.50/$10 per million tokens (OpenAI 2026a). Applying the documented high-detail image rules (OpenAI 2026b) to our prepared image dimensions gives 992,205 image tokens for 951 cases. If each case also uses 250 text-input tokens and 80 output tokens, the estimated bill is $3.835688. The assumed prompt lengths were not measured, and this scenario supplies neither a GPT-4o bill nor an accuracy result.
