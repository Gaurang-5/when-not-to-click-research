Graphical user interface (GUI) agents must decide not only where to click but whether the requested control exists and is uniquely identified. We audit a pinned native visual interface, Jev 1.14, that returns a visible-presence probability, a match-count distribution and one bounding box per request. We evaluate frozen selective-execution policies built from these scores. On held-out synthetic layouts, presence- and uniqueness-gated policies made no incorrect accepted clicks, but this did not transfer. A source audit showed that 200 of 951 human-labelled cases reused pilot screenshots. On the 751 independent real-interface cases, the three-signal policy accepted 26 cases with 61.5% conditional risk, and the frozen logistic gate accepted one case. Strict localization was correct for 41 of 131 unique targets (31.3%). This bounds any gate to about 6.1% coverage at 10% accepted-error risk. Real-data recalibration improved Brier loss but found no threshold meeting a 10% risk target. A matched-input quantized Qwen2.5-VL-7B baseline reached 16.8% strict accuracy. The contribution is a reproducible audit of transfer failure, denominator-sensitive safety metrics and cohort provenance, not a new uncertainty-control algorithm.

A screen coordinate answers where a model would click. It does not establish whether the requested control exists or is uniquely identified. Synthetic pilot. The pilot has 25 templates in five layout families, eight state variants per template, and procedural geometry labels. The splits are development (80 cases: sidebar and kanban layouts), calibration (40 cases: tables) and held-out test (80 cases: dialogs and toolbars). Each template contributes three unique cases and one case each of absent, multiple, occluded, offscreen and unclear. The synthetic experiment issued 200 calls without retries, and 199 parsed successfully. One development zero-width box failed closed.

Annotation. Two annotators independently labelled the same 951 cases without seeing model predictions. For each case they chose one of six target states, boxed all valid visible targets for unique and multiple cases, and recorded their confidence. Both corrected exports pass structural validation.

A deterministic rule flags a case if any of the following holds: status disagreement, box-count disagreement, matched IoU below 0.5, or low confidence. It flags 259 cases, and the reasons overlap. At the authors’ direction, the reviewer-approved Annotator B decision is final for flagged cases. The other 692 cases use consensus status and, where localizable, matched-coordinate means.

Source audit. An offline source audit found that 200 of the 951 human-labelled cases reuse exactly the same screenshot bytes and instructions as the pilot: 80 development, 40 calibration and 80 held-out cases. These cannot supply independent external evidence. We therefore report all 951 only as a mixed descriptive cohort, and separately analyse the 751 ScreenSpot-v2-variant real-interface cases (ScreenSpot-v2-variants n.d.; Figure 1).

The subset analysis was added after inspecting the mixed results, so it is an explicit post-hoc provenance correction, not a new preregistered experiment. The real subset consists of source-proposed negative instructions rechecked by humans; 131 were ultimately labelled unique, which shows why automatic negative labels were insufficient. This subset is not the standard positive-target ScreenSpot-v2 test set.

The main experimental policy definitions remain unchanged. We report strict localization (Equation 4); centre-only accuracy as a secondary metric; accepted-error risk and marginal error (Equation 6); state-specific acceptance; and useful automation among unique cases.

Confidence intervals use 2,000 nonparametric source-image cluster bootstrap resamples with seed 1729. Repeated instructions that share a screenshot are resampled together. Each ratio is recomputed after resampling; undefined replicates are omitted and their count is retained. Primary comparison tests flip paired source-cluster effects using 10,000 randomizations. Holm adjustment covers the absent and multiple endpoints within each cohort.

The real-only comparisons and all new subgroup, calibration, entropy and curve analyses are exploratory. The absent-target adjusted p = .0476 is borderline under this exploratory family and supports no central claim. The two-endpoint Holm adjustment does not cover the full collection of diagnostic analyses. Percentile bootstrap intervals are descriptive and can be unreliable with few accepted cases. A zero-error bootstrap interval is degenerate because resampling cannot create unseen errors; it is not evidence of a true zero risk.

The mixed-cohort zero-error logistic result is dominated by reused synthetic inputs: 68 of its 69 accepted cases come from the reused group. On the 751 real interfaces, E accepts one correct mobile case (0.13% coverage); D accepts 26 cases with 16 errors (61.5% risk); and B accepts 45 cases with 28 errors (62.2% risk). Reducing the absolute number of errors through abstention does not establish lower conditional risk or useful transfer (Figure 2).

For real unique targets, strict localization is correct in 41/131 cases (31.3%); IoU ≥ 0.75 occurs in 12/131 (9.2%); mean IoU is 0.327; and the secondary centre-only criterion is satisfied in 77/131 (58.8%). The large gap shows how sensitive apparent accuracy is to the scoring rule, so box acceptance and safe selection should be evaluated separately.

On real absent targets, B accepts 15/583 cases and D accepts 7/583, a case-weighted difference of −1.37 percentage points (exploratory two-endpoint Holm-adjusted cluster-sign p = .0476). On real multiple targets, acceptance changes from 1/29 to 0/29 (adjusted p = 1.0). The much larger mixed-cohort multiple-target reduction, from 21/54 to 0/54, comes mostly from reused synthetic examples, so its mixed-cohort significance does not establish a real-interface ambiguity result.

The completed repeat study reinforces this low-coverage result. Across five original-prompt passes, D accepts 21–26 real cases with 57.1–71.4% conditional risk, while E accepts 0–1 cases. Both prewritten prompt variants leave E with no accepts. These repetitions assess prediction stability; they do not increase the number of independent source images. Appendix C.4 reports all pass metrics, failures and additional cost.

Base localization and rejection quality are distinct. On real unique targets, strict accuracy is 31.3%, rising to 58.8% under a centre-only rule, and IoU ≥ 0.3 alone gives 48.9%. Among real cases that both annotators label unique, only 61.2% of their boxes meet IoU ≥ 0.5. These human agreement rates are a conditional reference, not an absolute performance ceiling. Full label and criterion sensitivity appears in Appendix C.1.

Probability quality. Original E has Brier loss 0.06125, worse than the retrospective constant-prevalence predictor at 0.05234, giving a Brier skill of −0.170 (Equation C1). E’s low expected calibration error (ECE) therefore does not establish useful probability quality. B and E have nearly identical AUROC and overlapping source-cluster intervals, so the extra native signals show no strong added discrimination on this real cohort. Appendix C.2 reports the full ranking and Brier analysis.

Real-data recalibration. A source-separated fit/calibration/test diagnostic trains a new logistic score on real fitting data only. Test Brier loss improves from 0.07345 to 0.05822. However, neither an empirical calibration-risk rule nor the fixed Learn-then-Test procedure finds a qualifying threshold at 10% accepted-error risk. This negative recovery result separates improved probability estimation from useful low-risk acceptance. The retrospective design and source-distribution assumptions preclude a new confirmatory guarantee; Appendix C.3 details the procedure.

Strata. The appendix reports strata by platform (desktop, mobile, web), status, target area, element type, instruction length, annotator confidence and label provenance. Target size is reference-box area divided by screenshot area, defined only for unique cases: small below 0.1%, medium 0.1–1%, large at least 1%. Annotator confidence is the lower of the two annotators’ confidences. Sparse strata are descriptive and are not used to tune policies.

Low entropy can mean confidence in absence or in multiplicity; it is not equivalent to confidence in a safe click. Figure 4 shows the full risk–coverage sweeps, and Figure 5 the reliability diagrams.

The independent Qwen2.5-VL-7B baseline uses the same 951 prepared images and human references. Input-image hashes and pinned model/protocol hashes match throughout. Primary accuracy uses only the 751 real cases, including 131 unique targets.

The two deployments differ: Qwen uses Q4_K_M quantization through Ollama, a frozen forced-box JSON prompt and prompted confidence values, while Jev uses its native typed interface. No Jev-trained logistic coefficients are applied to Qwen. The comparison is limited to these interface and deployment choices.

Strict accuracy is 31.3% for Jev and 16.8% for Qwen. The paired Qwen-minus-Jev strict difference is −14.5 percentage points (source-cluster 95% interval −22.2 to −7.3). Centre-only accuracy is closer, 58.8% versus 51.1%, and its difference interval reaches zero. The strict result favours the tested Jev interface on this cohort without establishing general model superiority (Figure 7).

A shared semantic rule accepts a valid output only when the predicted match-count label is one. It accepts 194/751 Jev cases with 163 incorrect accepts, and 604/751 Qwen cases with 583 incorrect accepts. This deliberately simple common rule is separate from frozen policies B–E, and shows that a semantic “one” label alone is insufficient for reliable acceptance. Prompted Qwen scores are not native calibrated probabilities.

Failures. The 62 Qwen failures are out-of-bounds or degenerate boxes and remain fail-closed; 57 occur in the real subset. Jev has 11 malformed outputs in the original matched 951 collection, all in the real subset. No output repair or selective rerun was applied. The comparison evaluates the specified forced-box interfaces; it does not isolate the effects of model weights, quantization, structured decoding or coordinate prompting, and different model-specific optimization may change the results.

Latency. Latency is wall-clock request-to-full-response time for each tested deployment. Hosted Jev and serial local Qwen use different hardware and execution stacks, so their observed times are not a hardware-normalised speed comparison. Both include malformed-response time. Local Qwen hardware and electricity are unpriced, so zero API charges do not imply zero economic cost. Published model numbers and GPT-4o price scenarios remain historical context in Appendix D.

Collection and cost. The external collection contains 951 HTTP-200 responses and 71 earlier credential rejections. Of the 951 responses, 940 parse and 11 fail closed (six invalid boxes and five invalid probability sums). Usage was extracted from raw responses even when parsing failed: 3,199,297 input and 73,284 output tokens. At the recorded Jev input rate of $0.042 per million tokens, with output tokens listed as free (TypeSafe AI 2026), estimated cost is $0.134370, about $0.0001413 per HTTP-200 response. This is a rate-based estimate rather than a provider invoice. Synthetic-study cost was approximately $0.019235. Mean HTTP-200 latency (valid or malformed) is 459 ms and p95 is 628 ms; including setup rejections gives a 456 ms mean.

The central result is the gap between authored-template discrimination and real-interface usefulness. Semantic confidence may reject obvious synthetic duplicates yet fail to transfer to underspecified real instructions and small controls. A low marginal error can be obtained through almost universal abstention. The source audit shows why an apparently strong mixed-cohort result must be decomposed before a generalization claim is made.

Annotation limitations. Status agreement is below 80%, and final labels for flagged cases follow reviewer-approved Annotator B rather than a separate third adjudicator, so independent review reliability cannot be inferred from this provenance alone. A blinded 100-case audit package (60 flagged and 40 consensus cases) is prepared for a human reviewer, but no third-review results are claimed. Changing labels later requires a new dataset version and a clearly marked sensitivity analysis.

Scope of the expansion. The reviewer-driven expansion adds a five-pass Jev study with two prompt variants and a pinned local Qwen2.5-VL collection; both are complete and reported in Appendix C. Native uniqueness and presence signals substantially improve selective clicking on authored synthetic layouts, but this does not establish real-interface usefulness. Removing the 200 reused pilot cases shows that the frozen logistic gate accepts just one of 751 real-interface cases, while three-signal conditional risk remains 61.5%. The defensible contribution is a transparent account of transfer failure, denominator-sensitive safety metrics and cohort provenance. On the 98 real cases that both annotators label unique, mean human-to-human IoU is 0.512. Box agreement reaches IoU ≥ 0.3 in 79.6% of cases, IoU ≥ 0.5 in 61.2% and IoU ≥ 0.75 in 19.4%. A’s box centre lies in B’s box in 81.6% of cases; the reverse direction is 88.8%. These conditional agreement rates are an annotation reference, not an absolute human or model ceiling; they exclude status disagreements and use no independently verified true boxes.

Low ECE alone is insufficient when correct localizations are rare. On all 740 valid real outputs, the constant prevalence is 41/740, whose Brier loss is 0.05234; original E has 0.06125 and skill −0.170. On the exploratory held-out valid subset, real-fitted E_real improves Brier loss to 0.05822, versus 0.07345 for the original E and 0.06616 for the calibration-prevalence constant — a 12.0% Brier skill relative to that constant. Probability estimation improves without demonstrating recovery of a 10%-risk automation policy.

AURC integrates right-continuous threshold risk over observed coverage; equal confidence scores enter together, preventing arbitrary within-tie ordering. Separate machine-readable results cover all cases (with parser failures placed below valid scores) and the unique-target valid subset. B and E have almost identical AUROC, and their overlapping intervals and similar AURC give no evidence that the extra native signals add strong discrimination on this real cohort. AUROC and AURC diagnose ranking, not calibrated risk or downstream harm.

Partition. Source images are assigned deterministically to fit, calibration and test partitions by a seeded hash, so no screenshot crosses partitions: fit has 296 cases in 292 source clusters, calibration 226 cases in 218 clusters, and test 229 cases in 220 clusters. E_real is a new L2 logistic diagnostic fitted on valid fit-partition outputs only; it does not replace the frozen original E.

Binomial guarantees target this sampled-cluster distribution and require independent, identically distributed source clusters and a fixed calibration protocol. Those assumptions cannot be fully established retrospectively, so certification here is a diagnostic property of the stated procedure rather than a deployment guarantee.

Empirical comparator. The empirical comparator maximises calibration acceptance subject to observed risk at most 10%, without a statistical guarantee. Neither it nor LTT finds a qualifying threshold for any of the six scores under the strict criterion, so the implemented recovery experiment does not show useful automation at the specified 10% risk target. An alternative risk budget or criterion would require an explicitly separate analysis and is not selected from test outcomes.

The source-selected test unit has 220 cases; full 229-case test metrics are also retained descriptively. Recalibration can improve probabilities and alter rejection thresholds, but it cannot create correct boxes that the grounder did not produce. At most 41/131 real unique cases satisfy the strict localization rule in the original pass. This separates weak base localization from a claim that rejection itself is wrong.

Protocol. The Jev repeat protocol adds four original-prompt passes on the 751 real cases (five passes including the existing one) plus two prewritten prompt variants, planning 4,506 additional requests. New requests use separate repeat/prompt IDs and immutable protocol hashes; non-auth attempted IDs, including timeouts and malformed outputs, are not silently resent. At manuscript generation, all 7/7 pass/variant cohorts are complete. Partial collections are not used for headline metrics.

Stability. Across the five original-prompt passes, correctness changes in 15/751 cases; the mean per-case modal match-count-label fraction is 0.976; the frozen accept decision changes for B in 26/751 cases, for D in 17/751 and for E in 1/751; and mean pairwise box IoU across 7,315 valid within-case output pairs is 0.711 (pairs involving malformed outputs excluded). Between-pass metrics and per-case consistency are retained separately; repeated observations are not pooled as independent screenshots.

Additional collection. The completed additional collection has 4,506 attempts, 67 fail-closed outputs, 16,457,448 input tokens and 346,928 output tokens. Including recorded usage from malformed responses, the additional rate-based cost estimate is $0.691213 (output tokens free at the recorded provider rate). No failed attempt was automatically repeated.

Pass results. Across the five original-prompt passes, B accepts 43–47 cases and D 21–26, with D conditional risk from 57.1% to 71.4%; E accepts 0–1 cases. The literal prompt yields 24 D accepts with 15 errors, and the conservative prompt 32 with 23. Prompt changes do not recover useful low-risk coverage under these frozen thresholds. These are descriptive within-case repetitions, not additional independent test screens.

Qwen baseline configuration. The same-input local baseline pins Qwen2.5-VL-7B (Qwen Team 2025) through Ollama with model digest recorded, Q4_K_M quantization, deterministic decoding with seed 1729, context length 4,096 and a maximum of 180 output tokens; the prompt and JSON schema were frozen before the first response. It requests a forced box plus verbalised presence, uniqueness and box-confidence scores, which are prompted self-reports rather than native Jev probabilities. Inference runs serially on an 18 GiB Apple arm64 Mac. Local compute and energy costs are unmeasured, so the API charge is not treated as the total economic cost.

The observed strict-accuracy ordering persists under each reference, while absolute performance, and even the number of unique targets, varies substantially with annotation choice. This is evidence for continued label audit, not proof that any reference is correct. Centre-only and IoU ≥ 0.3 variants are retained in the machine-readable sensitivity table.

Local Qwen request-to-full-response latency is 11.34 s mean and 16.67 s p95, including the cold first call; this measures the local quantized deployment, not a hosted Qwen service or a hardware-normalised speed comparison.
