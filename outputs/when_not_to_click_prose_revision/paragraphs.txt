0: When Not to Click: Auditing Native Visual Confidence under GUI Target Ambiguity and Distribution Shift
1: Dr. Sowmya K S, Garvit Mundra, Gaurang Bhatia
2: B.M.S. College Of Engineering
3: ABSTRACT
4: Graphical user interface (GUI) agents must decide not only where to click but whether the requested control exists and is uniquely identified. We audit a pinned native visual interface, Jev 1.14, that returns a visible-presence probability, a match-count distribution and one bounding box per request. We evaluate frozen selective-execution policies built from these scores. On held-out synthetic layouts, presence- and uniqueness-gated policies made no incorrect accepted clicks, but this did not transfer. A source audit showed that 200 of 951 human-labelled cases reused pilot screenshots. On the 751 independent real-interface cases, the three-signal policy accepted 26 cases with 61.5% conditional risk, and the frozen logistic gate accepted one case. Strict localization was correct for 41 of 131 unique targets (31.3%). This bounds any gate to about 6.1% coverage at 10% accepted-error risk. Real-data recalibration improved Brier loss but found no threshold meeting a 10% risk target. A matched-input quantized Qwen2.5-VL-7B baseline reached 16.8% strict accuracy. The contribution is a reproducible audit of transfer failure, denominator-sensitive safety metrics and cohort provenance, not a new uncertainty-control algorithm.
5: Keywords: GUI grounding; selective prediction; abstention; calibration; distribution shift; vision–language models
6: Introduction
7: A screen coordinate answers where a model would click. It does not establish whether the requested control exists or is uniquely identified. Missing, duplicated, occluded and offscreen targets create different decision problems. We study a pinned native visual interface that returns three things in a single request: a presence score, a caller-defined match-count distribution, and one bounding box. We address four research questions:
8: RQ1. Do native presence and uniqueness scores reduce incorrect accepted clicks relative to box confidence on held-out synthetic layouts?
9: RQ2. How well do the frozen policies transfer to human-labelled real interfaces?
10: RQ3. How do coverage, localization, calibration, latency and cost change across sources and target states?
11: RQ4. How does a frozen local vision–language model (VLM) baseline perform on the same images and human references?
12: The contribution is a reproducible audit of a native model interface, its transfer limitations and a matched-input baseline. We do not propose a new uncertainty-control algorithm.
13: The structure of the paper is as follows. “Related work” reviews prior work. “Materials and methods” defines the policies, data, annotation and evaluation protocol. “Results” reports transfer, calibration and model-comparison findings, which are interpreted in “Discussion”. “Conclusion” summarises the defensible claims.
14: Related work
15: OS-ATLAS develops cross-platform GUI grounding and action modelling (Wu et al. 2024). SafeGround uses stochastic spatial dispersion and calibration to control false discoveries (Wang, Fan, and Wang 2026). Argus compares uncertainty methods across models and interfaces, and finds that transfer depends on the observable regime (Kumar et al. 2026). CORA learns an action-risk guardian and calibrates execute/abstain behaviour (Feng et al. 2026).
16: Selective classification (Geifman and El-Yaniv 2017), Learn then Test (Angelopoulos et al. 2021) and Conformal Risk Control (Angelopoulos et al. 2024) supply the foundational rejection and risk-control framework. Qwen2.5-VL (Qwen Team 2025), ScreenSpot-Pro (Li et al. 2025) and UI-TARS (Qin et al. 2025) provide additional GUI-model and benchmark context. Together these establish prior art for abstention, calibration and GUI safety.
17: Our narrower evidence concerns three native scores from Jev 1.14 and the limitations of a synthetic-to-real evaluation. Published VLM performance is given as context in Appendix D rather than treated as an experiment on our labels.
18: Materials and methods
19: Native confidence and selective execution
20: Every primary-study request pins model version jev-1.14.0 with prompt presence-count-box-v1. Appendix C.4 separately specifies the repeat and prompt-variant protocol.
21: For case i, the interface returns three scores:
22: pᵢ ∈ [0, 1], the visible-presence confidence;
23: qᵢ = (qᵢ,₀, qᵢ,₁, qᵢ,>₁), a probability distribution over match counts (“Choice”), with uᵢ = qᵢ,₁ the probability of exactly one match;
24: bᵢ ∈ [0, 1], the box confidence attached to the predicted box B̂ᵢ.
25: The parser rejects malformed probabilities, unexpected model versions, non-finite coordinates and degenerate boxes. Let vᵢ = 1 if the output parses validly and vᵢ = 0 otherwise. A failed output counts as an abstention for every policy and stays in all evaluation denominators. The selected Choice label, argmaxₖ qᵢ,ₖ, is used only to route rejected cases to clarification or abstention. No interactive clarification was executed.
26: Each policy maps the native scores to an acceptance score g and compares it with a threshold τ (Table 1). Policy E is an L2-regularised logistic regression on the three native scores, with no interaction terms (Equation 1). Its coefficients β₀ = −4.496, βₚ = 2.509, βᵤ = 2.696 and β_b = 0.600 were fitted to the 79 valid development responses.

73: Data, annotation and provenance
74: Synthetic pilot. The pilot has 25 templates in five layout families, eight state variants per template, and procedural geometry labels. The splits are development (80 cases: sidebar and kanban layouts), calibration (40 cases: tables) and held-out test (80 cases: dialogs and toolbars). Each template contributes three unique cases and one case each of absent, multiple, occluded, offscreen and unclear. The synthetic experiment issued 200 calls without retries, and 199 parsed successfully. One development zero-width box failed closed.
75: Annotation. Two annotators independently labelled the same 951 cases without seeing model predictions. For each case they chose one of six target states, boxed all valid visible targets for unique and multiple cases, and recorded their confidence. Both corrected exports pass structural validation.
76: Status agreement is 759/951 (79.8%), with Cohen’s κ = 0.613.
77: Mean matched box IoU is 0.619 across 205 comparable cases.
78: A deterministic rule flags a case if any of the following holds: status disagreement, box-count disagreement, matched IoU below 0.5, or low confidence. It flags 259 cases, and the reasons overlap. At the authors’ direction, the reviewer-approved Annotator B decision is final for flagged cases. The other 692 cases use consensus status and, where localizable, matched-coordinate means.
79: Source audit. An offline source audit found that 200 of the 951 human-labelled cases reuse exactly the same screenshot bytes and instructions as the pilot: 80 development, 40 calibration and 80 held-out cases. These cannot supply independent external evidence. We therefore report all 951 only as a mixed descriptive cohort, and separately analyse the 751 ScreenSpot-v2-variant real-interface cases (ScreenSpot-v2-variants n.d.; Figure 1).
80: The subset analysis was added after inspecting the mixed results, so it is an explicit post-hoc provenance correction, not a new preregistered experiment. The real subset consists of source-proposed negative instructions rechecked by humans; 131 were ultimately labelled unique, which shows why automatic negative labels were insufficient. This subset is not the standard positive-target ScreenSpot-v2 test set.

112: Evaluation and uncertainty protocol
113: The main experimental policy definitions remain unchanged. We report strict localization (Equation 4); centre-only accuracy as a secondary metric; accepted-error risk and marginal error (Equation 6); state-specific acceptance; and useful automation among unique cases.
114: Confidence intervals use 2,000 nonparametric source-image cluster bootstrap resamples with seed 1729. Repeated instructions that share a screenshot are resampled together. Each ratio is recomputed after resampling; undefined replicates are omitted and their count is retained. Primary comparison tests flip paired source-cluster effects using 10,000 randomizations. Holm adjustment covers the absent and multiple endpoints within each cohort.
115: The real-only comparisons and all new subgroup, calibration, entropy and curve analyses are exploratory. The absent-target adjusted p = .0476 is borderline under this exploratory family and supports no central claim. The two-endpoint Holm adjustment does not cover the full collection of diagnostic analyses. Percentile bootstrap intervals are descriptive and can be unreliable with few accepted cases. A zero-error bootstrap interval is degenerate because resampling cannot create unseen errors; it is not evidence of a true zero risk.

191: Calibration and decision analysis
192: Base localization and rejection quality are distinct. On real unique targets, strict accuracy is 31.3%, rising to 58.8% under a centre-only rule, and IoU ≥ 0.3 alone gives 48.9%. Among real cases that both annotators label unique, only 61.2% of their boxes meet IoU ≥ 0.5. These human agreement rates are a conditional reference, not an absolute performance ceiling. Full label and criterion sensitivity appears in Appendix C.1.
193: Oracle coverage bound. For fixed recorded boxes and labels, let π̂_corr = (1/n) Σᵢ vᵢ yᵢ be the fraction of all cases with a strictly correct localization. Any gate accepts correct cases at a rate of at most π̂_corr (Equation 7a). If the gate’s observed conditional risk is at most a tolerance α, its total coverage is bounded by Equation (7b).
196: Here π̂_corr = 41/751 = 0.0546, so even an oracle gate is bounded by 0.0546/0.90 ≈ 6.1% coverage at α = 0.10 (Figure 3). This is a deterministic finite-cohort bound, not a population guarantee or a human ceiling; a different grounder or scoring rule changes it.
198: Figure 3. Frozen real-cohort operating points (coverage, conditional risk; Table 3) against the oracle frontier of Equation (7b). For a fixed set of recorded boxes, no gate can reach the shaded region, because only 41 of 751 cases are strictly correct. At α = 0.10, coverage is bounded by 6.1%. The observed policies sit far above the frontier, so better gating alone cannot recover useful low-risk automation.
199: Probability quality. Original E has Brier loss 0.06125, worse than the retrospective constant-prevalence predictor at 0.05234, giving a Brier skill of −0.170 (Equation C1). E’s low expected calibration error (ECE) therefore does not establish useful probability quality. B and E have nearly identical AUROC and overlapping source-cluster intervals, so the extra native signals show no strong added discrimination on this real cohort. Appendix C.2 reports the full ranking and Brier analysis.
200: Real-data recalibration. A source-separated fit/calibration/test diagnostic trains a new logistic score on real fitting data only. Test Brier loss improves from 0.07345 to 0.05822. However, neither an empirical calibration-risk rule nor the fixed Learn-then-Test procedure finds a qualifying threshold at 10% accepted-error risk. This negative recovery result separates improved probability estimation from useful low-risk acceptance. The retrospective design and source-distribution assumptions preclude a new confirmatory guarantee; Appendix C.3 details the procedure.
201: Strata. The appendix reports strata by platform (desktop, mobile, web), status, target area, element type, instruction length, annotator confidence and label provenance. Target size is reference-box area divided by screenshot area, defined only for unique cases: small below 0.1%, medium 0.1–1%, large at least 1%. Annotator confidence is the lower of the two annotators’ confidences. Sparse strata are descriptive and are not used to tune policies.
202: Entropy baselines. For an exploratory entropy baseline, we use the normalised certainty h(qᵢ) of the match-count distribution (Equation 8), with the convention 0 ln 0 = 0. A second baseline, “entropy-plus-one”, uses h(qᵢ) · 𝟙[argmaxₖ qᵢ,ₖ = 1], i.e. it additionally requires the selected Choice to be one. Thresholds for both use only original synthetic calibration outputs under the same rule (Equation 2), but these baseline designs were introduced after seeing transfer outcomes.
204: Entropy alone selects τ* = +∞ and accepts nothing, because the tied high-score calibration mass exceeds 30%.
205: Entropy-plus-one uses τ* = 1 and accepts two real cases, one of them in error.
206: Low entropy can mean confidence in absence or in multiplicity; it is not equivalent to confidence in a safe click. Figure 4 shows the full risk–coverage sweeps, and Figure 5 the reliability diagrams.

232: Utility framing. As standard decision-theoretic framing rather than a methodological contribution, we define a transparent utility: a correct accepted click earns one unit, an incorrect accepted click loses λ > 0 units, and a rejection earns zero. This is a hypothetical sensitivity model, not measured harm; dollar API cost and wall-clock latency are reported separately. With TP = Σᵢ aᵢyᵢ and FP = Σᵢ aᵢ(1 − yᵢ), the utility is Equation (9).
234: On real interfaces, Û_B = (17 − 28λ)/751, Û_D = (10 − 16λ)/751 and Û_E = 1/751. The pairwise break-even points are:
235: D exceeds B only when λ > 7/12;
236: E exceeds D only when λ > 9/16;
237: E exceeds B only when λ > 4/7.
238: The ranking is therefore B > D > E for λ < 9/16, and E > D > B for λ > 7/12. A completely abstaining policy has zero utility. These break-even points expose the coverage–risk trade-off rather than labelling one policy universally best (Figure 6).

241: If a score q(x) were a calibrated probability of correctness, the expected-utility (Bayes) rule would accept exactly when Equation (10) holds. The native scores have not been shown to satisfy this calibration assumption, so Equation (10) supplies no guarantee for D or E.
243: Zero-error observations. Under optimistic independent Bernoulli sampling, zero errors in m accepted cases yields the exact one-sided 95% upper confidence bound in Equation (11). For the single real E acceptance (m = 1), this bound is 95%, which shows how weak that zero-error observation is. Source dependence makes even this illustration optimistic.

Centre-only accuracy is closer, 58.8% versus 51.1%, and its difference interval reaches zero. 273: A shared semantic rule accepts a valid output only when the predicted match-count label is one. It accepts 194/751 Jev cases with 163 incorrect accepts, and 604/751 Qwen cases with 583 incorrect accepts. This deliberately simple common rule is separate from frozen policies B–E, and shows that a semantic “one” label alone is insufficient for reliable acceptance. Prompted Qwen scores are not native calibrated probabilities.
274: Failures. The 62 Qwen failures are out-of-bounds or degenerate boxes and remain fail-closed; 57 occur in the real subset. Jev has 11 malformed outputs in the original matched 951 collection, all in the real subset. No output repair or selective rerun was applied. The comparison evaluates the specified forced-box interfaces; it does not isolate the effects of model weights, quantization, structured decoding or coordinate prompting, and different model-specific optimization may change the results.
275: Latency. Latency is wall-clock request-to-full-response time for each tested deployment. Hosted Jev and serial local Qwen use different hardware and execution stacks, so their observed times are not a hardware-normalised speed comparison. Both include malformed-response time. Local Qwen hardware and electricity are unpriced, so zero API charges do not imply zero economic cost. Published model numbers and GPT-4o price scenarios remain historical context in Appendix D.
276: Collection and cost. The external collection contains 951 HTTP-200 responses and 71 earlier credential rejections. Of the 951 responses, 940 parse and 11 fail closed (six invalid boxes and five invalid probability sums). Usage was extracted from raw responses even when parsing failed: 3,199,297 input and 73,284 output tokens. At the recorded Jev input rate of $0.042 per million tokens, with output tokens listed as free (TypeSafe AI 2026), estimated cost is $0.134370, about $0.0001413 per HTTP-200 response. This is a rate-based estimate rather than a provider invoice. Synthetic-study cost was approximately $0.019235. Mean HTTP-200 latency (valid or malformed) is 459 ms and p95 is 628 ms; including setup rejections gives a 456 ms mean.
277: Discussion
278: The central result is the gap between authored-template discrimination and real-interface usefulness. Semantic confidence may reject obvious synthetic duplicates yet fail to transfer to underspecified real instructions and small controls. A low marginal error can be obtained through almost universal abstention. The source audit shows why an apparently strong mixed-cohort result must be decomposed before a generalization claim is made.
279: Annotation limitations. Status agreement is below 80%, and final labels for flagged cases follow reviewer-approved Annotator B rather than a separate third adjudicator, so independent review reliability cannot be inferred from this provenance alone. A blinded 100-case audit package (60 flagged and 40 consensus cases) is prepared for a human reviewer, but no third-review results are claimed. Changing labels later requires a new dataset version and a clearly marked sensitivity analysis.
280: Cohort limitations.
281: The real cohort is skewed toward absence (583/751), and unique labels arise from rechecking proposed negative instructions.
282: Mobile, web and desktop are represented, but the rare offscreen and occluded strata are too small for stable conclusions.
283: Jev’s public training data are not known, so training contamination cannot be ruled out.
284: Repeated template screenshots are addressed by source-image clustering, but application-level dependence may remain.
285: The new subgroup diagnostics were planned after mixed outcomes were visible and have no global multiplicity correction.
286: Scope of the expansion. The reviewer-driven expansion adds a five-pass Jev study with two prompt variants and a pinned local Qwen2.5-VL collection; both are complete and reported in Appendix C. The single quantized baseline does not establish robustness across model families or deployment configurations. The present work supplies no formal risk control under distribution shift. Offline incorrect clicks are not measurements of actual user harm, and no real application action was executed. Stronger future evidence requires independent adjudication, new balanced real-interface cases and broader model and configuration coverage.
287: Conclusion
288: Native uniqueness and presence signals substantially improve selective clicking on authored synthetic layouts, but this does not establish real-interface usefulness. Removing the 200 reused pilot cases shows that the frozen logistic gate accepts just one of 751 real-interface cases, while three-signal conditional risk remains 61.5%. The defensible contribution is a transparent account of transfer failure, denominator-sensitive safety metrics and cohort provenance. The matched-input quantized Qwen baseline shows weaker strict localization under the specified interface, and neither semantic one-label rule yields reliable acceptance. Broader model superiority, formal safety and practical utility remain unestablished.

1162: Partition. Source images are assigned deterministically to fit, calibration and test partitions by a seeded hash, so no screenshot crosses partitions: fit has 296 cases in 292 source clusters, calibration 226 cases in 218 clusters, and test 229 cases in 220 clusters. E_real is a new L2 logistic diagnostic fitted on valid fit-partition outputs only; it does not replace the frozen original E.
1163: Learn then Test. For Learn then Test (Angelopoulos et al. 2021), the threshold grid is fixed at Λ = {0, 0.05, …, 1}, i.e. 21 thresholds. For threshold λⱼ ∈ Λ, let Kⱼ be the number of accepted calibration cases and Eⱼ the number of incorrect ones. We test the null hypothesis that conditional accepted-error risk is at least α = 0.10 with the one-sided exact binomial lower-tail p-value in Equation (C2), where δ = 0.05 and M = 126 (21 thresholds × 6 scores) applies Bonferroni correction. To address within-screenshot dependence, one case is independently hash-selected from every source cluster, using no labels. Certified thresholds maximise calibration acceptance within the passing family; if none passes, the procedure abstains.
1165: A threshold with Eⱼ = 0 passes only if 0.9^Kⱼ ≤ 0.05/126, i.e. only with Kⱼ ≥ 75 error-free accepts.
1166: Binomial guarantees target this sampled-cluster distribution and require independent, identically distributed source clusters and a fixed calibration protocol. Those assumptions cannot be fully established retrospectively, so certification here is a diagnostic property of the stated procedure rather than a deployment guarantee.
1167: Empirical comparator. The empirical comparator maximises calibration acceptance subject to observed risk at most 10%, without a statistical guarantee. Neither it nor LTT finds a qualifying threshold for any of the six scores under the strict criterion, so the implemented recovery experiment does not show useful automation at the specified 10% risk target. An alternative risk budget or criterion would require an explicitly separate analysis and is not selected from test outcomes.

1199: Protocol. The Jev repeat protocol adds four original-prompt passes on the 751 real cases (five passes including the existing one) plus two prewritten prompt variants, planning 4,506 additional requests. New requests use separate repeat/prompt IDs and immutable protocol hashes; non-auth attempted IDs, including timeouts and malformed outputs, are not silently resent. At manuscript generation, all 7/7 pass/variant cohorts are complete. Partial collections are not used for headline metrics.
1200: Stability. Across the five original-prompt passes, correctness changes in 15/751 cases; the mean per-case modal match-count-label fraction is 0.976; the frozen accept decision changes for B in 26/751 cases, for D in 17/751 and for E in 1/751; and mean pairwise box IoU across 7,315 valid within-case output pairs is 0.711 (pairs involving malformed outputs excluded). Between-pass metrics and per-case consistency are retained separately; repeated observations are not pooled as independent screenshots.
1201: Additional collection. The completed additional collection has 4,506 attempts, 67 fail-closed outputs, 16,457,448 input tokens and 346,928 output tokens. Including recorded usage from malformed responses, the additional rate-based cost estimate is $0.691213 (output tokens free at the recorded provider rate).
